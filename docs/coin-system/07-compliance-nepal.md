## 1. Bottom line

**The coin is almost certainly not a payment instrument. The VAT treatment of the
redemption is settled and it is in our favour. The thing that can put you in prison
is the Privacy Act.**

Three things dominate everything else in this document:

1. **VAT is answered, and the answer is good.** A Nepali chartered accountant has
   confirmed that unlocking a study resource, video lecture, or mock test in exchange
   for coins is an **exempt educational service** under Schedule 1, and that the
   company is **not required to register** for VAT. Every coin therefore carries no
   output VAT, so the coin economics are not margin-negative. Two constraints come
   with the answer and both are live: while we stay exempt-only we can never recover
   input VAT on anything we buy (§2.3.1), and the coin catalogue must stay entirely
   inside the educational perimeter (§2.3.1).
2. **Privacy Act 2075 s.26(1) is criminal** — up to three years' imprisonment, with
   the Government of Nepal as plaintiff — for handing student personal data to a
   third party without consent. There is **no data-protection regulator, no adequacy
   mechanism, no DPO duty, and no safe harbour.** A bundled "I agree to the terms"
   checkbox is the weak point in the typical implementation.
3. **Most of your market is under 18.** Privacy Act 2075 s.33 requires guardian
   consent *and* a benefit test for minors. A referral flow inherently discloses a
   minor's data to another person.

One finding cuts against the earlier research in this project: **the design lane's
statute guess was half right.** It cited "Consumer Protection Act 2075 (2018)" — the
act and year are correct. The section was wrong (§18 rather than §16(2)) and the
statute name was garbled. Corrected below.

## 2. VAT — VAT Act 2052

### 2.1 Is a coin a supply?

**We believe issuance is at most part-consideration for the eventual service, not a
separate supply. Confidence: MEDIUM.** No IRD ruling, circular, or Nepali case law
on loyalty points under the VAT Act 2052 was located — Nepal has no developed
precedent culture on this point. Do not assume India's answer transfers; it is a
different statute under a different regulator.

Definitions, VAT Act 2052 s.2(1):

| Term | Statutory text | Effect on coins |
|---|---|---|
| Goods (e) | "both movable and immovable property" | A coin is a contractual access right, not property → falls out |
| Service (f) | "anything other than goods" | Absurdly wide; a coin *would* qualify if it is a supply at all |
| Supply (g) | "the act of selling, exchanging transferring of any goods and services **or the act of licensing or the act of contracting by taking consideration**" | The "contracting by taking consideration" limb is the exposure |
| Consideration (h) | "anything to be obtained for cash, goods, service or value" | The trailing "or value" catches a non-cash inducement to complete a profile or upload a resource |

**The argument that saves you:** a coin is not something that can be "sold,
exchanged, transferred, licensed", and it is not money or a money-equivalent. It
creates no obligation to pay anything. The most defensible characterisation is an
**internal unit of account within a single supply relationship**, with the taxable
event at redemption. **Confidence MEDIUM — analysis on retrieved statutory text, no
ruling located.** This characterisation is now load-bearing, because the redemption it
points at is the exempt supply, so it is item 3 on the confirmation list in §2.6.

### 2.2 Redemption, not issuance, is the taxable event

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** s.5(1)(a) levies VAT on "goods and services supplied within
Nepal"; s.5(2) on "the value of each taxable transaction". No service is supplied
when a coin is granted — the student receives a number. The service is supplied when
the resource is delivered. **This is a timing/nexus conclusion, not a Nepali
authority. UNVERIFIED as a stated position.**

### 2.3 The rate question, answered: the redemption is exempt

**A Nepali chartered accountant has concluded that unlocking a study resource, video
lecture, or mock test in exchange for coins is an exempt educational service under
Schedule 1, and that the company is not required to register for VAT.** The statutory
analysis below is kept rather than deleted, because the conclusion rests on a narrow
reading of the exemption and because the constraints in §2.3.1 follow directly from
it. **Confidence: HIGH on the position as the CA states it. The reasoning behind it
has not been received in writing, so this is an answer we hold, not a document we
own.**

- **Standard rate:** s.7(5) as amended — "this Act shall be in **single rate of 13
  percent**".
- **Exempt:** Schedule-1, Group 6 (Education), verbatim:
  > "(a) Research related acts in schools or universities.
  > (b) Vocational or professional trainings or refresher trainings **provided by institutions fully owned by the Government of Nepal**.
  > (c) **Educational services provided by schools and universities**."
- **Zero-rated:** Schedule-2. `UNVERIFIED` whether any education service appears; the
  consolidated reprint shows Schedule-2 as export-oriented. Assume none.
- **No 0% "online education" category is in force**, as far as verifiable. A
  commercial aggregator states the position as binary — SaaS and digital services
  to Nepali consumers at 13% — but that is a **non-Nepali-primary source, indicative
  only**.

**Finance Act 2083 materially supports the exemption failing rather than widening.**
Per U.R.P. & Associates CA: VAT goes multi-rate, and **"Schedule-1 (the exempt list)
[was] fully replaced."** The items added to the exempt list are jute, wood
briquettes, herbal medicines, bandage, X-ray preparations, clinical trial kits,
diagnostic reagents, digital financial service fees, and insurance-agent services.
**Education is not among them.** No education rate was created. **This paragraph is
retained as recorded analysis, not as the conclusion.** It is part of why the earlier
reading of the statute pointed away from the exemption, and the CA has concluded
otherwise. That tension is exactly why the written reasoning is the top item in §2.6.

> ⚠️ **Critical gap.** Because Schedule-1 was *fully replaced*, the **post-2083
> gazetted Schedule-1 could not be retrieved.** The Group 6 wording quoted above is
> from the pre-amendment consolidated Law Commission reprint. **Re-verify against the
> current Finance Act 2083 gazette before relying on it.** This is still open, and it
> is on the confirmation list in §2.6.

**Education Equity Fee — a levy to ask about.** U.R.P. report a **3% Education
Equity Fee on private schooling, college, university and technical-institute charges
from 1 Shrawan 2083 (Schedule-7)**, corroborated by Nepali financial press. The levy
is drafted against institutional charges, which a SaaS vendor is not, so it likely
does not reach you directly — but if you invoice a college, the *college* pays it on
its own charge, and whether it cascades to third-party edtech vendors is unclear.
**Ask your CA.** See §2.6.

**Is Finance Act 2083 in force?** Yes with reasonable confidence — gazetted
**30 Ashadh 2083**, effective **1 Shrawan 2083 = 16/17 July 2026**. Note the CA
commentary was written at *bill* stage; by convention finance bills pass unamended,
but treat the detail as professional analysis, not gazette text.

### 2.3.1 Three consequences of the settled answer

**A. Exempt means we can never register, so input credit is gone for good.** Under
the rule quoted in §2.4, a person doing only Schedule-1 transactions "cannot register
itself" for VAT. Because the redemption is exempt, that is us. So input VAT on any
future purchase (cloud, tooling, vendors, equipment) is **never recoverable, for as
long as we stay exempt-only**. **This is not a new problem and not a regression. We
are unregistered today, so the status quo is unchanged; nothing is being given up
that we currently have.** The correct way to read it is that the exemption does not
cost us anything we had, and it buys the thing we needed.

**B. Crossing the registration threshold re-opens the question, under time
pressure.** The moment turnover reaches the threshold, registration becomes
mandatory, the 30-day clock starts, and exempt-versus-taxable has to be settled again
before we comply. That is a **growth trigger to diarise, not a date**, and it should
be checked quarterly rather than waited for. The Rs 10 lakh business-loan trigger in
§2.4 is the other way in, and it is the more dangerous of the two because it has
nothing to do with trading.

**C. The coin catalogue must stay inside the educational perimeter.** This is the
sharpest of the three, because it is a design constraint rather than a legal
opinion. Our exemption rests on a **narrow reading**: our content is an "educational
service" within Schedule 1 Group 6(c), whose wording covers *"educational services
provided by schools and universities"*. That reading is only safe while **everything
the coin system unlocks remains educational**. So the coin catalogue is not free to
drift. Adding a non-educational revenue line near the coin system risks tipping the
business into a mixed position, and a mixed position could change the classification
and put the whole economy back in question. **Anything a coin can unlock must be a
study resource, a video lecture, or a mock test, and nothing else.**

### 2.4 Are you even VAT-registered?

**Answered: no, and the CA confirmed we are below threshold on both triggers.**
Turnover is below the Rs 30 lakh services threshold, and no business loan over Rs
10 lakh has been taken. Neither trigger is met, so registration is not required.
**Confidence: HIGH**, and HIGH specifically on the thresholds (Baker Tilly, Nepal Tax
Fact 2025/26):

> "…If the turnover … exceeds **Rs. 30 lakhs** in last 12 months where it is
> **service** business or **mixed** business of goods and services… The person shall
> register for VAT **within thirty days**."

**Monitor the Rs 10 lakh business-loan trigger.** It is the one that can arrive
without warning, because it has nothing to do with trading. A business loan
exceeding Rs 10 lakh obliges registration regardless of turnover, so it would change
the position overnight and put the exemption question straight back on the table under
a 30-day clock. **Any raise or working-capital facility goes past the CA before it is
signed, not after.**

**The lock-out, now confirmed rather than hypothesised.** "Person doing only the
transactions of items listed in Schedule 1 … **cannot register itself** for Value
Added Tax." The CA's conclusion that the redemption is wholly Schedule-1 exempt puts
us inside that lock-out. We are not registered, we cannot register while we stay
exempt-only, and input credit is therefore unavailable to us permanently. See §2.3.1.

Digital Service Tax at 2% applies only to **non-resident** providers and not to a
resident Nepal SaaS.

### 2.5 Breakage: still open, and now an accounting question

- **Blocked input credit is moot.** s.17 is confirmed as the credit section, but its
  sub-clauses were never retrieved, and the question no longer bites: we cannot
  register, so there is no input credit available to be blocked. This comes off the
  launch checklist as a VAT question and stays only as an unretrieved-text gap in §9.
- **Breakage (expiry of unredeemed coins): `UNVERIFIED GAP`, still open.** No Nepali
  primary source — VAT Rules 2053, IRD circular, Finance Act schedule, or case law —
  was located on breakage or expiry-based recognition. Confidence MEDIUM-LOW that
  expiry is not a supply, on the reasoning that it is the expiry of a contractual right
  rather than the provision of a service. **Ask your CA rather than treating it as
  answered.**

  **The question has changed character, not been closed.** With no VAT to owe, expiry
  is now an **accounting** question rather than a tax one: what happens to the coin
  liability and to recognised revenue when a lot expires unredeemed. That treatment
  is in §6, and it belongs to the auditor rather than the tax adviser. The residual
  VAT-adjacent risk is unchanged in size but no longer in scope: an IRD officer could
  still argue that expiry of a valuable instrument is a deemed supply of value, and
  that argument only bites if we are ever required to register.

## 3. Income Tax Act 2058

### 3.1 Students pay no tax on coins — but the same sentence hides a trap

**We believe: no tax on the recipient, because they are not carrying on a business
or profession, and gifts/scholarships are an enumerated exemption. Confidence:
MEDIUM-HIGH.** ICAN Advanced Taxation material, on s.10 exempt income:

> "Amount Received as Gift, Bequest, Inheritance, or Scholarship (except as required
> to be included in calculating the taxable income from **business under Section 7**,
> from **employment under Section 8** and from **investment under Section 9** or
> **wind-fall gains under Sec. 5/88A**)"

**The trap is the carve-out.** "Windfall gain" is defined as *"lottery, gift, prize,
baksis, award for winning (jitauri) and any other type of windfall gains"*, taxed
under **s.5 / s.88A**.

> ⚠️ **This is a naming constraint, not a copy preference.** If your marketing calls
> coins a "prize" or an "award", you have handed IRD the word that triggers windfall
> gain tax. **Never use: prize, award, win, winner, raffle, draw, baksis, jitauri.**
> Use: coins, points, credits, balance, unlock, reward.

`s.7(2)(e)` also treats a "Prize or gift in connection with business" as business
income — which should not bite on a student recipient, but would if you ever paid
uploaders a cash revenue share.

### 3.2 If you ever pay money out

**Confidence: HIGH on s.88, MEDIUM on the carve-out.**

- **s.88(1)**: "In making payment by a resident person for interest, natural resource,
  rent, royalty, **service charge** having source in Nepal … the person shall
  withhold tax at the rate of **Fifteen percent** of the total amount of payment."
- **s.89**: withholding on contract/contraction — the natural home for a uploader
  revenue-share agreement.
- **s.90(3) deemed withholding — the reason to keep paying in coins:** a person
  obliged to withhold under s.87, 88, 88A or 89 who fails to do so is treated as
  having withheld, and **IRD can assess the tax against you directly.**
- Service charge invoiced on a VAT bill: 1.5%. Commission to a **non-resident**:
  5% — a live trap if you ever engage offshore content partners.

## 4. Payment instruments — the central bank

**The governing statute is NOT the Nepal Rastra Bank Act 2055.** For payment-system
purposes it has been replaced by the **Payment and Settlement Act, 2075 (2019)**, made
under the NRB Act, effective 18 March 2019. NRB's Payment Systems Oversight Report
2023/24 Box 4 confirms this and lists the current framework.

**NRB's own words on your exact mechanic** — Payment Oversight Report 2023/24, §6
"Access on Payment Instruments":

> "…digital payment instruments like Mobile Banking, Cards, QR code, connectIPS,
> e-Wallets… **loyalty and redemptions are increasingly becoming popular in Nepal**.
> Furthermore, **the licensed institutions** are constantly working to modernize and
> innovate payment instruments."

Read that carefully. NRB (i) places "loyalty and redemptions" **inside** the
payment-instrument category, and (ii) attributes them to **"the licensed
institutions"** — the BFIs/PSPs in Annexes 1–4 (eSewa, Khalti, Fonepay, City Wallet,
Sulav Pay, and similar). **NRB contains no carve-out, exemption, FAQ, or licence class
for a non-financial company's self-issued closed-loop loyalty points. That absence is
the finding.**

So the closed-loop position is an argument from **absence of a licence class**, not
from an exemption. That is weaker than India's position and it is why ADR-001
enforces the invariant **in code** rather than relying on a policy document.

Non-bank eligibility exists in principle — every Nepali digital wallet is a private
company holding a PSP licence — but all are *financial-purpose* wallets with bank
float. **There is no precedent in the NRB licence register for a non-financial
closed-loop loyalty programme.** That suggests NRB has never been asked, which also
means there is **no established path for you to be approved.**

### 4.1 Switch points

| Change to the coin | Consequence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **As designed** — earned only, no cash in, no cash out, no transfer, no third-party acceptance, no chance element | Strongest available argument to be outside: not money, not e-money, no float, no settlement, no clearing participation, no PSP licence. Bolstered by NRB framing loyalty/redemption as a *licensed-institution* activity you are not. | **Proceed**, with the VAT and privacy fixes in §2 and §6 |
| **Purchasable with cash** | Escalates hard. Buying an instrument with money is the defining feature of a prepaid instrument. PSP licence, incorporation, mandatory paid-up capital, settlement-bank agreement, full float backing, KYC/AML, annual system audit, 5-year licence. | **Do not do.** If ever: NRB licence first, **budget 12–18 months** |
| **Cash-outable / convertible to NPR** | Maximum escalation. A direct currency-exchange function: e-money territory, float backing, AML/KYC, suspicious-transaction reporting, possible Money Laundering Act exposure | **Prohibited on this model. Do not build the capability** |
| **Transferable user↔user (P2P)** | Creates a *payment system between users*. Also hits the pyramid prohibition — see §5.3 | **Prohibited** |
| **Usable at third parties** | Open-loop instrument; maximum perimeter; settlement-bank agreement required | **Out of scope. This is why "closed loop" is load-bearing** |
| **Redeemable for a "reward" of goods or cash-equivalent** | Income Tax Act 2058 "prize"/"award for winning" → windfall gain to the recipient. Also CPA 2075 s.16(2)(d) on pricing that includes "the price or cost of any contest, lottery, occasion" | **Never convert a coin into a "reward"** |
| **Earned by friends who themselves refer** | CPA 2075 s.16(2)(p) + s.18(e) "token system" — see §5.3 | **Keep referrals strictly single-level, one generation** |

**Escalation path, concretely:** incorporate under the Companies Act 2006 → obtain
NRB **Letter of Intent** → apply within **6 months** → inject mandatory paid-up
capital → execute a **settlement-bank agreement** and file the signed copy to NRB
**within 7 days** → NRB field inspection within **30–60 days** → pay a **1%
issued-capital security deposit** plus fees → licence issued, **valid 5 years**,
renewal at least 3 months before expiry. Also note the **Unified Payment System
Directive, 2082 (April 2026)** is the current controlling instrument for limits and
security, and its three pillars are full float backing, KYC/AML, and annual system
audit. *Confidence MEDIUM-HIGH on the process; UNVERIFIED against the Act's own
section numbers.*

> **India, as a labelled comparative only:** the RBI position on closed-loop
> non-transferable loyalty points not being payment instruments is **not determinative
> of Nepal's position** and must not be cited to a Nepali regulator as authority.

## 5. Privacy Act 2075 (Act No. 6 of 2018) — and Consumer Protection Act 2075

### 5.1 Privacy: the cross-border vendor question, answered

Full operative text retrieved (Nepal Law Commission reprint).

**You are covered.** s.2(d) defines "body corporate" to include a company
"established under the prevailing law", and s.26(1) binds "a public body **or body
corporate**". The Act is not limited to government. (Note s.2(e) "public body" is a
*different, wider* list — s.27's processing prohibition binds only public bodies, so
a private edtech is outside it unless gazetted under s.2(e)(9). Useful, but do not
rely on it if you take government grants.)

**Your entire student record is personal information.** s.2(c) expressly lists
"(1) caste, ethnicity, birth… (2) **His or her education or academic qualification**,
(3) His or her address, telephone or… email, (4) His or her passport, citizenship
certificate… (6) **His or her thumb impressions, fingerprints, retina of eye, blood
group or other biometric information**".

**The operative provisions:**

- **s.26(1)**: "…the personal information collected by or remained under the
  responsibility or control of a public body **or body corporate** shall not be used
  or **given to any one** without the **consent of the concerned person**"
- **s.12(2)–(4)**: consent at collection; data "**used only for the purpose for which
  such data have been collected**"; and no provision of listed data to anyone else
  without consent — the list includes **(e) biometric details** and (h) business or
  transaction data.

> ⚠️ **s.23 is not your operative provision.** It is awkwardly drafted around "official
> authorized under law" and is much weaker for a private company. **s.12 and s.26 are
> what bind you.** Flag the ambiguity to counsel.

**The answer on cross-border transfer:** there is **no localisation requirement** for
private companies. UNESCAP/UN-ECA's Nepal profile (data as of January 2026): "Nepal
imposes no bans on cross-border data transfers and no local-processing mandates for
personal data" — a localisation duty applies only to **OTT** providers. The regime is
conditional: transferring personal information to a third party **requires consent**.

⇒ **Shipping fingerprints, name, phone, and education profile to Fingerprint Pro /
SEON / Sift / Kount / Castle (all US/EU-hosted) is permitted in Nepal, but only on
specific, informed, purpose-limited consent.** You have no adequacy clause, no SCC, no
DPIA, no DPO, and no breach-notification regime to fall back on. **The only thing
standing between you and a criminal conviction is the quality and scope of the
consent.** There is also no regulator to ask for comfort.

**Penalties.** s.29(1)(s) makes a contravention of s.26(1) an offence; s.29(2) sets
punishment at "**imprisonment for a term not exceeding three years or fine not
exceeding thirty thousand rupees or both**"; s.29(3) provides that the **Government of
Nepal shall become the plaintiff**, adding the offence to Schedule-1 of the National
Criminal Procedure Code 2074. s.31 allows the victim to claim **compensation**.

> ⚠️ **The NPR 30,000 maximum fine is trivially small. The exposure is the
> imprisonment and the compensation claim, not the fine.** Design accordingly.

**What does NOT exist** (each a gap you must fill yourself, because there is no legal
baseline to lean on): no data-protection regulator or authority; **no retention
schedule**; **no breach-notification duty**; no DPO, DPIA, or records-of-processing;
no privacy-by-design; no data-subject portability or erasure. Rights that do exist:
access (s.25), rectification (s.28, with the proviso that it may be refused if the
person "has already taken any advantage or facility on the basis of such
information"), complaint (s.30, District Court, within 3 months), compensation
(s.31).

**In force today:** Privacy Act 2075 (effective 18 September 2018) + Individual
Privacy Regulation 2077 (2020). **No comprehensive Data Protection Act exists.**
⚠️ Search results here are contaminated — a "PDP Law effective 1 Jan 2026" article is
**Singapore's** PDPA, a "Personal Data Protection (Amendment) Ordinance 2026" is
**Bangladesh's**. Neither applies to Nepal. **Pending and material:** the Information
Technology and Cyber Security Bill 2082 (Parliament register: "Distribution to member
Present"), whose s.80 would codify exactly the consent and purpose-limitation rules
this design depends on. **Assume it will land; design for it now.**

### 5.2 The under-18 problem

**s.33, verbatim:**

> "…if the matters … are related to **the person who has not attained the age of
> eighteen years** … such matters may be published or privacy thereof be disclosed
> **only when his or her guardian or curator gives consent and if it benefits him or
> her**…"

**Most of the +2 / Grade 12 / early-bachelor's / competitive-exam audience in Nepal is
under 18.** A referral flow inherently discloses a minor's data to another person. You
need verifiable guardian consent **and** a benefit test for every under-18 account —
which is also a hard UX problem, because guardian consent does not scale.

**Recommendation: restrict the referral earn to 18+ accounts** with a self-declaration,
and say so in the terms. That is the cheap, defensible design. Build a guardian flow
only if the referral mechanic demonstrably works among adults.

### 5.3 Consumer protection — a pyramid-scheme trap in a coin feature

**Correction to the brief: the operative statute is the Consumer Protection Act 2075
(2018), not the 2054 (1997) Act, which is superseded.** (The design lane's act/year
guess was correct; its section was not.)

**Four limbs of s.16(2) land directly on this feature:**

1. **s.16(2)(c)(3) is the functional Nepali analogue of a free-claims rule**:
   "advertising … in false and misleading manner, while selling the goods or services
   **even when no benefit is obtained as declared**." If you advertise a benefit and
   then require a payment, subscription, auto-renewal, or ad exposure, this is the
   clause. **Practical rule: if there is any money or commitment on the other side, do
   not write "free."**
2. **s.16(2)(n) is the mandatory-disclosure rule** — a professional service provider
   must "specify the **price**, quality of service, venue and time". **You must publish
   the coin↔resource conversion rate, what is included, and the expiry.** Hiding the
   rate is a designated unfair-trade act.
3. **s.16(2)(p) + s.18(e) are a pyramid-scheme prohibition, and "token system" is
   named in the statute.** s.18(e): "operating service by adopting a **circle system,
   quota system, rotational system, trip system or token system**, in cooperation with
   more than one business persons or business groups that provide any service of the
   same nature." s.16(2)(p): "selling or providing by **setting up levels or series**
   more than the business level prescribed." **A multi-level referral tree is not a
   grey area here.**
4. **s.16(2)(d)**: pricing that includes "the price or cost of any **contest, lottery,
   occasion**" is prohibited — which is why no chance mechanic is permissible.

**The product name contains the word "Token", and that is a considered risk, not an
oversight.** The feature has been named **StudsToken**. On the substance the
s.18(e) prohibition cannot apply: the statutory trigger is operation "in cooperation
with more than one business persons or business groups that provide any service of the
same nature", and our mechanic is single-level, non-transferable, non-purchasable, and
involves no cooperating businesses at all. There is no multi-level structure, no
downline, and no commission flow.

The risk is optical rather than legal. A regulator, a plaintiff's lawyer, or a
journalist reading "StudsToken" next to a statute that names a "token system" will
form a first impression the facts do not support, and a complaint does not require
merits to be expensive. A single-level referral programme is lawful under s.16(2)(p)
and s.18(e), but it will attract one question it would rather not answer in public.

**Mitigation, in order of preference:**

1. **Do not use "token" in any compliance-facing, press-facing, or terms-facing
   surface.** The name is fine in product UI where it is uncontroversial, and fine
   in internal documentation. It is the statutory register that is a problem.
2. **State the single-level structure proactively** in the published coin table
   required by s.16(2)(n), so the first thing a reader finds is the structure, not the
   name.
3. **Never describe the referral mechanic in network, chain, downline, tree, or
   level language.** Those are the words that map onto s.16(2)(p) and s.18(e), and
   using them would be doing the regulator's work for them.
4. **If it becomes a visibility problem, rename.** It is cheap: internal identifiers
   (`coin_account`, `internal/coins`) are unaffected, so a product rename is a
   find-and-replace in copy only.

Confidence that the mechanic is outside s.18(e): **HIGH on the substance**, MEDIUM on
how a Nepali enforcement body would read the name.

**Penalties — the misleading-advertising band is the top one.** s.38(f) deems
contraventions of s.16(2)(b), (c), (d), (e) and (p) to be offences; s.40(3)(b) punishes
them with "**imprisonment from two years to five years or fine from four hundred
thousand rupees to six hundred thousand rupees or both**." s.40(1)(b) punishes a
contravention of s.18 — the circle/token-system prohibition — with 3 months to 1 year
and/or NPR 100,000–300,000. s.39 allows an Inspection Officer to impose **on-the-spot
fines** during market monitoring, doubled on repeat, with appeal to the Director
General within 7 days on a 50% cash deposit.

**Enforcement is via the Ministry of Industry / Department of Commerce, Supplies and
Consumer Protection**, and the Government of Nepal is the plaintiff. **The Consumer
Court under s.41 has apparently never been constituted** (`UNVERIFIED`, single
secondary source) — so a consumer-court suit is unlikely, but the Inspection Officer
route is live and **requires no court**.

**Unfair contract terms:** there appears to be **no general UCT invalidation provision**
in CPA 2075 (confidence MEDIUM; I read the prohibited-activities chapter). The Act's
substitute is **transparency** (s.16(2)(n)) plus **anti-deception** (s.16(2)(a)–(c)).
So: publish the coin table, publish the expiry, notify material changes in-product and
by notice, and **never reprice coins already issued** — retrospective repricing
converts a transparent deal into s.16(2)(a) "misleading the actual price".

### 5.4 Is this a lottery?

**We believe: no, on four factors — but this is the most unsettled item in the memo
and deserves one paragraph from a Nepali advocate. Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM.**

There **is** a lottery statute: the **Lottery Act, 2025 (1968)** with **Lottery Rules,
2027 (1971)**, requiring Government approval via the Ministry of Home Panchayat,
sample lotteries, published winning numbers, and an audit of income and expenditure.
⚠️ `UNVERIFIED and important` — whether it is still in force after 58 years is
unconfirmed; the Rules reference the Panchayat system (pre-2015). **Confirm before
launch.** The concept is alive regardless, because CPA 2075 s.16(2)(d) regulates
contest/lottery pricing.

**Four factors placing you outside a lottery:** no chance element (redemption is
deterministic); no stake of money (coins are not purchased); no draw, ticket, or
published winner; and the "prize" is access to a service you already sell.

**The factor pulling the other way:** coins have an **exchange value**, so the moment
you add a chance mechanic — a lucky multiplier, a scratch card, "100 coins → mystery
prize" — **you have created a lottery.** **Do not build chance mechanics. This is a
design constraint, not a legal opinion, and it is the easiest thing in this document
to violate by accident via an A/B test.**

### 5.5 E-Commerce Act 2081

**We believe you must register with the Department of Commerce, Supplies and Consumer
Protection and display it. Confidence: MEDIUM** (thin but consistent secondary
evidence, plus working precedent: established Nepali e-commerce operators disclose
this registration in their own T&Cs). `UNVERIFIED` — the gazette text was not
retrieved. **Obtain it.** The **Electronic Transactions Act 2063 (2008)** also remains
relevant, and the **Social Networks Directives 2023** impose electronic-transaction
penalties (up to NPR 50,000 and/or 6 months' imprisonment for privacy breaches) —
relevant if referral links are run through social media.

### 5.6 Copyright — bigger than the tax risk

**Do not rely on the teaching exception for the core product. Confidence: HIGH on the
statutory text.** Copyright Act 2059 s.18 permits reproduction "for teaching and
learning activities" without authorisation, but only:

> "(a) To reproduce **a small portion** of any published work by way of citation …
> (b) To reproduce, broadcast and exhibit **some portions** of the work for purposes
> of educational activities **to be performed in the classroom**"

and s.18(2) requires copies to "indicate the source and the author's name". Meanwhile
s.7(a) reserves to the author the exclusive right "To reproduce the work" and
s.7(e)/(k) the right to "sell, distribute or rent the original and copy" and
"communicate the work to the general public".

A commercially-exploited online past-paper bank is (i) not "in the classroom", (ii)
not "a small portion" when a full paper is reproduced, and (iii) prejudicial to the
author's economic right. **The teaching exception does not cover this business model.**

Note for context: "NEB notes / past papers / model questions" is an established and
apparently unlicensed commercial category in Nepal, which is precisely why the exposure
is live. `UNVERIFIED` — no Nepali case law or NEB position on copyright in
question-syllabus papers was located. Assume infringement is possible; enforcement
would be a complaint to the **Copyright Registrar**, and the Act provides for seizure
of copies (s.32). **Commission original questions for paid tiers.**

**Never imply NEB / HEB / CHE / TU or any university endorsement.** False-or-
misleading-advertisement exposure under CPA 2075 s.16(2)(b) is the **top penalty band
(2–5 years)**. Footer disclaimers do not cure a claim in the marketing copy.

## 6. Accounting

**Establish the framework before writing any ledger policy. Nepal has NOT adopted
IFRS 15.** Under **NFRS 2013** the applicable revenue standard is **NAS 18: Revenue**
(an IAS 18 analogue), and **IFRIC 13: Customer Loyalty Programmes** is on the adopted
IFRIC list. Under ICAN's earlier mandatory list the revenue standard is **NAS 7**
(also IAS 18). Which binds you depends on whether you are an NFRS or an NAS entity —
**ask your CA/auditor, because the answer changes the model.**

**Recommended posture for a pre-revenue or early-revenue Nepal SaaS:** expense reward
credits as a **promotional cost at grant** if they are not a material performance
obligation; if material, defer as a **contract liability** measured at expected
redemption value and release on redemption. Recognise **breakage into revenue only
when it is highly probable the points will not be redeemed** (IFRIC 13 / IFRS 15
breakage logic as the analytical analogue — **labelled as analogue reasoning, not a
Nepali requirement**). Document the policy as a **judgement** with the redemption-rate
data behind it.

> The biggest practical accounting risk is the **balance sheet**, not the P&L: an
> ever-growing unredeemed balance with no liability recognised understates your
> obligations and misleads investors. **Get the auditor to sign the treatment in
> writing** before you publish numbers.

## 8. Do-not-do

1. **Do not make coins purchasable with money.** PSP licence territory; 12–18 months.
2. **Do not make coins cash-outable or convertible to NPR.** Currency-exchange
   function; e-money territory.
3. **Do not make coins transferable, giftable, or P2P.** Creates a payment system
   between users, and CPA 2075 s.18(e) expressly prohibits a "token system" and
   s.16(2)(p) prohibits "levels or series" — a **pyramid-scheme prohibition, not a
   tax question.**
4. **Do not make the reward outcome random.** No scratch cards, lucky multipliers,
   draws, or mystery rewards. Engages the Lottery Act permission regime and
   s.16(2)(d). **The easiest constraint in this document to violate by accident via
   an A/B test.**
5. **Do not use the words prize, award, lottery, baksis, jitauri, win, winner, raffle,
   or draw.** "Windfall gain" under Income Tax Act 2058 is defined to include them
   and is taxed under s.5/88A. Use coins/points/credits/balance/unlock.
6. **Do not call anything "free"** if there is any money or commitment on the other
   side. CPA 2075 s.16(2)(c)(3) — 2 to 5 years' imprisonment under s.40(3)(b).
7. **Do not ship a fraud vendor on a bundled consent checkbox.** s.26(1) is criminal,
   up to 3 years, GoN as plaintiff, and there is no regulator and no safe harbour.
   **No vendor → no problem. Bundled consent → problem.**
8. **Do not collect referral data from under-18s** without guardian consent and a
   benefit test. Restrict referral earn to 18+ instead.
9. **Do not reuse data across purposes in code.** s.12(3).
10. **Do not silently reprice coins already issued** or change their expiry without
    conspicuous notice. s.16(2)(a)/(n).
11. **Do not leave an unbounded unredeemed balance with no liability recognised.**
12. **Do not reproduce NEB/HEB past papers on a commercial scale relying on the
    Copyright Act 2059 s.18 teaching exception** — it covers "a small portion" and
    "classroom" activities. Commission original questions.
13. **Do not imply endorsement** by NEB, HEB, CHE, TU, or any university.
14. **Do not launch a multi-level referral tree.** Single level, one generation.
15. **Do not pay student uploaders cash without a withholding memo.** s.88 (15%),
    s.89 (contract), and s.90(3) deemed withholding.
16. **Do not add a non-educational reward or revenue line next to the coin system.**
    The exemption is the reason the coin economics work, and it holds only while
    everything a coin unlocks is educational. See §2.3.1.
17. **Do not proceed on this memo alone.** The §2.6 confirmations, items 16 and 27–30
    in §7, and the gaps in §9 are `UNVERIFIED`. **Get a Nepali CA and a Nepali
    advocate on them before launch.**
